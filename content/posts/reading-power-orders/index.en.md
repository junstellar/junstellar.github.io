The final episode. We started at [Episode 1](/en/p/ai-power-hunger/) with "AI grows by eating electricity" and walked through nine installments, using the words **order backlog** an awful lot along the way — Hyosung Heavy's 17.5 trillion won, the big three's 30-odd trillion combined, "four to five years of work."

Today we dig into that term properly. And at the end I'll share the most important thing I learned writing this series.

## First, what happened in Q2 2026

A real case beats a textbook. This happened last month.

![LS Electric +17.26% versus HD Hyundai Electric −13.05%, opposite reactions in the same quarter](same-quarter-opposite.png)

**LS Electric** reported Q2 operating profit of 178.5 billion won (+64%) on July 23 — a record quarter, with 2.1 trillion won of new orders. The stock closed **up 17.26%** that day at 223,500 won.

**HD Hyundai Electric** reported Q2 operating profit of 287 billion won (+37.3%) on July 28. That's a larger absolute figure than LS Electric's, and it met consensus. Yet the next day the stock fell as much as **13.05%** intraday.

Same quarter, same industry, both good results. Why the opposite outcome?

*(To be straight about it, July 29 was the KOSPI circuit-breaker day covered in [Episode 9](/en/p/korea-power-stock-map/), so the whole market fell hard. But the drop was far larger than the index's, and analysts and the press pointed to a specific reason.)*

**What sank HD Hyundai Electric was one line on the conference call.** Management said revenue from its new distribution business would be recognized **from 2029 onward.** It also noted the Q2 tariff refund benefit was only about 6 billion won, with meaningful improvement roughly two years out. The market's disappointment was about *when* the money arrives, and brokerages cut targets immediately — Shinhan from 1.5 million to 1 million won, Samsung from 1.53 million to 1.16 million, Daishin from 1.5 million to 1.15 million.

LS Electric, by contrast, delivered a record quarter with orders stepping up a level, and brokerages raised targets (KB from 260,000 to 280,000 won; Korea Investment to 330,000).

**The lesson: the market doesn't ask "how much did you earn?" It asks "was it better than expected?" and "when does it arrive?"** It's exactly the principle from semiconductor [Episode 10](/en/p/reading-earnings/), where Samsung announced 89 trillion won of operating profit and the stock still fell.

## What "order backlog" actually means

Three terms to separate.

![The three stages from new orders through backlog to revenue, and the lag in power equipment](order-to-revenue.png)

- **New orders**: contracts newly won this quarter — a *reservation* of future revenue.
- **Order backlog**: the portion not yet recognized as revenue — the work stacked up.
- **Revenue and profit**: money actually booked on the income statement.

The arithmetic is simple.

> **Backlog = last quarter's backlog + this quarter's new orders − this quarter's revenue recognized**

Here's what beginners often get wrong: **a shrinking backlog is not automatically bad.** It can simply mean revenue recognition outpaced new orders — that stacked-up work is converting into actual money.

Power equipment also carries a **long lag.** An extra-high-voltage transformer takes about three years from order to delivery, and some large units up to four. US transformer lead times went from 143 weeks in 2024 to 160 weeks by Q1 2026. In other words, **an order won today is revenue three years from now.** What [Episode 3](/en/p/transformer-supercycle/) called a lead-time bottleneck returns, for an investor, as the gap between expectation and results.

## But is a bigger backlog always better?

Throughout this series I've treated backlog as a positive signal. In this final episode, let me turn it over.

![Four things to watch out for when reading order backlog](backlog-traps.png)

**① Backlogs contain low-margin orders too.** A big number can hide thin profit. One commentary notes that top global firms rarely carry more than three years of backlog, while Korean firms tend to tout three to five years — which can mean margin was conceded to win volume. **So always read backlog size alongside the operating margin trend.**

**② Contracts get cancelled and delayed.** Work sitting in the backlog can vanish if the customer's situation changes. Shipbuilding has seen large contracts unilaterally terminated for geopolitical reasons.

**③ Margin arrives on a lag.** A good example is Taihan Cable from [Episode 7](/en/p/copper-cable-ai/). While copper spiked in 2025 its operating margin was squeezed to 1.5%; once cost-escalation clauses passed the price increases through, Q1 2026 margin jumped to 5.6%. Same company, same business — the lag between raw material and selling price decided the margin.

**④ Companies count it differently.** What goes into the backlog varies by firm, so cross-company comparison is risky.

None of which means backlog is useless. Korean accounting research has found that disclosed backlog information carries significant predictive power for future earnings. **Used well it's a good leading indicator; read as a single number it becomes a trap** — that's the accurate statement.

## How to check it yourself

Reading a news article and reading the filing are different things. It takes five minutes.

![Five steps to check order backlog in the electronic disclosure system](how-to-check.png)

1. Search the company name on Korea's **electronic disclosure system (dart.fss.or.kr)**.
2. Filter to **periodic disclosures** and open the annual, quarterly or semi-annual report.
3. In the left-hand table of contents, click **"II. Business Overview" → "Sales and Order Status."** You'll find order totals, delivered amounts and backlog by business segment.
4. **Read three numbers together** — new orders, backlog, and operating margin.
5. **Compare against guidance.** Calculate what percentage of the company's annual order target the cumulative new orders represent.

Step five is surprisingly powerful. In 2026 alone, HD Hyundai Electric filled 43% of its $4.22 billion annual target in Q1; Hyosung Heavy raised its target from 8.4 trillion to **12 trillion won**; LS Electric lifted its from 4 trillion to 6–6.5 trillion. **A guidance raise is one of the events share prices react to most**, and tracking the run rate lets you anticipate it.

Note that large contracts also appear as **single supply contract** disclosures. But these are only mandatory above 5% of prior-year revenue on the main board (2.5% for large corporations) and 10% on KOSDAQ — so **smaller contracts never show up.** LS Electric disclosed about 800 billion won of Q2 orders, but including undisclosed contracts the real figure was estimated above 2 trillion. Counting only disclosures understates the business.

## Investor's view — pulling it together

**① Expectations beat absolute numbers.** 287 billion won is larger than 178.5 billion, and yet the shares went the other way. Consensus and guidance outweigh the headline figure.

**② Always ask "when."** What broke HD Hyundai Electric wasn't the result but a date: 2029. Attach "how many years until this is revenue?" to every order you read about.

**③ Margin direction, not margin level.** HD Hyundai Electric's 25.1% operating margin is top-tier. But improving only 0.2 percentage points from the prior quarter read as fading margin momentum. The market watches the slope more than the height.

**④ Open the original filing at least once.** Figures in articles are usually the ones a company wants emphasized. Five minutes checking orders, backlog and margin together gives you a much more accurate picture.

## Summary

- **Backlog = prior backlog + new orders − revenue recognized.** A declining backlog isn't necessarily bad. In power equipment, orders take **three to four years** to become revenue.
- **The market reads expectations and timing, not absolute amounts.** In Q2 2026 LS Electric gained 17.26% on a record quarter and an order step-up; HD Hyundai Electric fell 13.05% despite meeting consensus, because its new business revenue starts in 2029.
- **Never judge on the backlog number alone.** Low-margin orders, cancellations, the lag between input costs and pricing, and differing counting conventions all hide inside it. Read backlog **with operating margin**, and check **guidance achievement.**

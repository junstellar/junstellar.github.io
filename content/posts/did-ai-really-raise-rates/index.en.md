A confession first.

In [part 4 of the rotation series](/en/p/is-bio-a-rate-play/) I quoted the figure that AI companies had grown from 2.4% to 16.4% of the US investment-grade bond market, and noted the press calling this **"reverse crowding out"** — companies pushing government bonds aside rather than the other way round. This series exists because that link seemed too good to leave alone.

**Today I'm going to doubt it.**

## The power went out at my house last night

As it happens, the neighbours installed a large air conditioner a few days ago. The thought arrives on its own: *is it them?*

Then you step outside in the morning and find **the whole neighbourhood was dark.** The air conditioner drops instantly from prime suspect to one load among many. It did draw more power. It just doesn't explain this.

This episode is that morning walk.

## First, what actually happened

![US term premium and the 30-year yield](term-premium-jump.png)

On 17 August the **US 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.31%**. The next day it touched **5.337%** intraday — **the highest since 2007, nineteen years.**

One concept is worth having here: the **term premium**.

> **Term premium** = the extra yield lenders demand for parting with money for a long time.
> Nobody knows what the next decade holds, so they want to be paid for that uncertainty.

It matters because **when the term premium rises, long rates rise no matter what the central bank does.** This is why the Fed can cut and the long end refuses to follow.

And this number went from **0.2% in May 2025 to 1.20% in May 2026** — a full **percentage point** in a year.

So: who lifted it?

## There are three suspects

Gather the reporting and the same three culprits keep appearing.

![The three suspects behind higher long rates](three-suspects.png)

### Suspect 1 — the US fiscal deficit

The biggest and oldest of them.

**The cumulative deficit through July of fiscal 2026 had already exceeded the entire fiscal 2025 deficit of $1.775 trillion** — with two months of the year still to run. Fitch projects the US deficit at **7.4% of GDP** across 2026–2027.

Deficits are covered by issuing Treasuries. More supply means lower prices and higher yields. And higher yields mean heavier interest costs, which widen the deficit further. One calculation making the rounds: **one dollar in every five of federal revenue now goes to interest.**

### Suspect 2 — Japan

The quietest suspect, and the most interesting.

**Japan's 30-year government bond yield hit a record 3.52%**, with the 10-year at 2.88%. This in a country whose yields sat at zero for a generation.

Why does that touch US rates? Because **Japan is the largest foreign holder of US Treasuries, with more than $1 trillion.**

Put yourself in a Japanese institution's position. When domestic bonds paid nothing, buying Treasuries was worth it even after currency hedging costs. But **if your own 30-year now pays 3.5%**, the reason to cross the Pacific and carry the currency risk gets thinner.

Layer on the **yen carry trade** — borrowing cheaply in yen to buy higher-yielding assets elsewhere. When Japanese rates rise, that trade unwinds and assets get sold worldwide.

**And this suspect has nothing to do with AI.**

### Suspect 3 — AI corporate bonds

The one everybody points at.

Goldman Sachs, tallying on 5 August, put **this year's AI-related debt issuance at roughly $500 billion**. Hyperscalers — Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle — account for about 40% of it. Their global bond issuance rose from **$108 billion last year to $194 billion this year**.

Large numbers. But size only means something next to another number.

## Put the sizes side by side

![Fiscal deficit versus AI debt issuance](size-comparison.png)

| Item | Size |
|---|---|
| US fiscal deficit (cumulative through July, FY2026) | **over $1.775 trillion** |
| AI-related debt issuance this year (as of 5 Aug) | **about $500 billion** |

**The deficit is more than three and a half times larger** — and it had two months left to run.

The comparison isn't perfect. The counting periods differ, and not all deficit financing goes into ultra-long maturities. But the direction is unambiguous: **the biggest borrower in the bond market is still the US government.**

## The decisive alibi

The clinching evidence wasn't about size, though.

**Over the same period, long-term government yields in Germany and France also hit their highest levels in 15 to 30 years.**

Germany has no hyperscalers. Neither does France. No data center bonds are elbowing government paper aside in those markets. Yet their long yields moved the same direction at the same time as America's.

**The whole neighbourhood was dark.**

AI corporate bonds don't explain that. It calls for fiscal expansion across the developed world, inflation worry, and a shift in the belief that **long-duration bonds are safe at all.** Middle East tension lifting oil prices and tariff policy added price pressure, and the Bank of Japan's policy turn pushed the term premium along.

## That doesn't make AI innocent

Stopping here would be half the job. The test has to run in the other direction too.

![Change the maturity bucket and the answer changes](where-ai-shows.png)

**AI-related companies accounted for 40% of US investment-grade corporate bonds issued this year with maturities of fifteen years or longer.**

That number bends the argument back. Across the whole corporate bond market AI is one issuer among many; in the longest maturity bucket it is **two out of five**. For scale, US investment-grade issuance in 2025 was $1.499 trillion, the second-largest year on record.

Why the ultra-long end? Because data centers are twenty- and thirty-year assets. Borrow short for a long asset and you have to refinance at every maturity, so you borrow long from the start. It is the flip side of the mismatch problem from [part 2](/en/p/datacenters-off-the-books/).

And the buyers of ultra-long government bonds and ultra-long corporate bonds are **the same people**: pensions and insurers, matching thirty-year liabilities with thirty-year assets. When AI bonds pour into that narrow market, **a genuine contest for space does break out there.**

## So the conclusion

Here's how I'd rewrite what I said in part 4 of the rotation series.

> **AI corporate bonds are not the sole culprit behind higher rates. They are one of three, and probably the smallest.**
> **But at the longest maturities they are unmistakably an accomplice.**

Where you look changes the answer. Measured against the whole rise in the 10-year, blaming AI is an exaggeration. Measured at the 30-year corporate issuance window, it isn't.

One footnote: the Bank of America estimate I cited in part 4 — that corporate bonds and mortgage securities explain about **30 basis points** of this year's 10-year move — I could not re-confirm while researching this piece. So the argument above rests only on figures I verified directly.

## Where this reaches Korea

**Here Korea gets uncomfortable.** The 30-year Korean treasury hit a record **4.751%** in August.

Korea has no hyperscalers either. No AI corporate bonds are crowding out government paper in the domestic market. So why did our yields rise?

Apply the same logic and there are two possibilities: **US rates transmitted straight through, or domestic factors of our own.** **Part 5** will work that out — whether American long rates really arrive here intact, or whether fiscal and supply conditions at home matter more.

Before that, **part 4** enters the most contested ground in this series: Nvidia's residual value guarantees and circular financing. Is it the same structure telecom equipment makers ran in 1999, or is that comparison lazy?

## Summary

- **The 30-year Treasury touched 5.337% intraday on 18 August, the highest since 2007.** The 10-year term premium went from **0.2% to 1.20% in a single year.**
- **Three suspects**: ①the US fiscal deficit ②rising Japanese yields and fading foreign demand ③AI corporate bonds. That's the combination the reporting names.
- **On size, the deficit dominates.** Cumulative FY2026 deficit through July already exceeded **$1.775 trillion**; AI-related debt issuance was **about $500 billion**. More than three and a half times apart.
- **The decisive alibi is Germany and France.** Long yields there hit **15-to-30-year highs** with no hyperscaler in sight. The whole neighbourhood was dark.
- **Still not innocent.** **40% of US investment-grade corporate bonds issued this year at fifteen years or longer were AI-related.** In that narrow market, bought by pensions and insurers, a real contest for space exists.
- **Conclusion: AI is one of three culprits and probably the smallest — but an unmistakable accomplice at the long end.**

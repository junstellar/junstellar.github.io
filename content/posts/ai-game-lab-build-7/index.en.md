After the cosmic evolution of [the last post](/p/ai-game-lab-build-6/), game number seven is a **shunting yard**. It's a 3D puzzle where you push and pull wagons until the train is made up in the right order. 🚂

👉 **[Go play the shunting yard yourself](/games/shunting/)**

Half of this post isn't really about the game — it's about **how badly I got lost building it**. I threw more than 60 AI agents at it, and there was a round where the score actually went *down*. The reason had nothing to do with what I expected.

## It started with someone else's game

In late July I saw the **Claude of Duty** story. Matt Shumer gave Claude Opus 5 a single prompt and got back an entire browser FPS. Three.js and WebGL2, roughly 55,000 lines across 11 subsystems, and **not one external asset**. Textures, meshes, sounds — all generated by code at runtime. It picked up 2,381 GitHub stars in five days.

I wasn't so much envious as **curious whether it actually works.** I wanted to try it.

I had no intention of copying the FPS, though. Making another one of the same thing is pointless, and more to the point, I don't really know shooters. So I went looking for a subject, and I came across a game ad on Naver Blog (Korea's big blogging platform): a parking lot packed with cars that you pull out with arrows — one of those ubiquitous mobile puzzle ads.

I asked what genre that even is, and it turned out to be an unblock puzzle (the Rush Hour family) mixed with a sort puzzle in a single ad. So I said, "give me five variations that aren't parking." Out came a shunting yard, a light maze, unscrewing bolts, elevator dispatch, and untangling yarn.

I picked the **shunting yard**. It's a subject almost nobody has done as a Korean browser game, and I liked that it works out to pure logic with no physics engine.

For the art direction I settled on a "floating diorama." The yard sits on a clod of earth torn out of the ground, hanging in the sky. Grass and ballast on top, rock strata and dangling roots underneath. I narrowed the camera to a 24° field of view so it reads not as a world at real scale but as **a model sitting in your palm**. The cover image at the top is the result.

## The prompt I gave

This is all of it. From here on the agents ran on their own.

The prompt I actually typed was in Korean, so here it is as written, with an English rendering below.

> 조차장 아이디어를 가지고 해당 분야 최고의 게임을 만들어 달라.
>
> 모든 면에서 완벽하고 **시각적으로 아름다워야** 하며, 텍스처부터 물리 효과를 비롯해 생각할 수 있는 모든 요소를 **AAA급 품질**로 구현해야 합니다. 여러 하위 에이전트를 병렬로 투입하고, 각 에이전트가 개별 요소를 하나씩 담당하게 하여 게임을 완벽하게 만들어 주세요. 각 요소를 반복해서 개선하고, **별도의 하위 에이전트가 시각적으로 검토**하여 AAA급 품질에 도달했는지 확인하게 하세요. 검토 에이전트는 **매우 엄격하게** 평가해야 하며, 결과물이 **AAA급으로 보이지 않는다면** 만족할 때까지 계속 개선해야 합니다.
>
> 각 하위 에이전트가 실제 `oskarstalberg.com/Townscaper` 나 `krunker.io` 이런 게임들과 비교했을 때 그 품질에 완전히 감탄할 때까지 멈추지 마세요. **실제 게임 화면과 나란히 놓고 블라인드 테스트**를 진행하여 어느 쪽이 더 좋아 보이는지 판단할 수 있을 정도여야 합니다. 구현 방법에 제한은 없고 현재 PC의 모든 자원을 이용해도 좋으니 완벽해질 때까지 반복해서 개선하세요. 하위 에이전트들을 병렬로 적극 활용하고 최고 수준의 코딩 역량을 투입하세요. 이 개발에 필요한 모든 권한을 승인한다.

In English:

> Take the shunting-yard idea and build the best game there is in that space.
>
> It has to be perfect in every respect and **visually beautiful**, with everything you can think of — from textures to physics effects — implemented at **AAA-grade quality**. Run several subagents in parallel, each one owning a single element, and make the game perfect that way. Iterate on each element repeatedly, and have **a separate subagent review it visually** to confirm it has reached AAA quality. The review agent must grade **very strictly**, and if the result **doesn't look AAA-grade**, keep improving until it's satisfied.
>
> Don't stop until every subagent is genuinely in awe of the quality compared to real games like `oskarstalberg.com/Townscaper` or `krunker.io`. It should hold up well enough to run **a blind test side by side with real game footage** and judge which one **looks better**. There are no constraints on how you implement it and you may use every resource on this PC, so iterate until it's perfect. Lean hard on parallel subagents and bring your best coding ability. I grant all the permissions this build needs.

As you can see if you read it, every word in that prompt that points at quality **is about looking**. Visually beautiful, review it visually, if it doesn't look AAA-grade, side by side to see which one **looks better**.

Not once does it say "it has to be playable." That fact comes back later, exactly as stated.

## What the game is

A shunting yard is where freight trains get rearranged. Arriving wagons are split up by destination and made up into new trains. The work is called **shunting**.

The game is that, more or less literally. There's an order slip at the bottom of the screen: "on the departure track, in this order" — covered wagon, tank wagon, hopper. Line up the wagons scattered across the sidings on the departure track in that order and the level is clear.

<video src="play.mp4" poster="poster.webp" autoplay muted loop playsinline controls preload="metadata" style="width:100%;max-width:960px;border-radius:12px;display:block;margin:1.4rem auto 0.6rem;"></video>

*A full clear of the first level (3× speed). Go to the siding and couple up → push onto the departure track → cut → the locomotive pulls out alone. Three moves.*

There are **only two controls**.

**Tap a track.** The locomotive goes there. On arrival it **automatically couples to every wagon** standing on that track. That's one move.

**Cut a coupling.** You can cut anywhere in the consist. Everything behind the cut stays where it is; the locomotive takes only what's in front. **It costs no moves.**

That's it. No push button, no pull button, no speed control.

## Two rules, so why is it hard

A siding is a **dead end**. There's a buffer stop at one end, so it has exactly one door. In data-structure terms it's a **stack**. The wagon you put in first ends up trapped at the back, and things come out in reverse order.

Which means that to change the order, you **have to use another siding as temporary storage.**

The second level teaches nothing but that. It's called "Two Wagons, Wrong Way Round."

```
Start:  Siding 1 │ 🟥 🩶            Goal:  Departure │ 🩶 🟥
```

Red and grey are standing in order, and the order slip wants the opposite. Couple up and drop them as-is and the order doesn't change. You have to park one on another siding, put the other one on the departure track first, then go back for the one you parked. That's **par 5**.

There's one more constraint on top. The **headshunt** — the short stretch of track you must pass through whenever you move between sidings. It holds a fixed number of vehicles.

From level 8 that number tightens to 3. Meaning you can't drag five wagons out in one go. **You have to move them in batches.** That's where the difficulty jumps.

And one last thing. Even with every wagon placed, **the level doesn't end while the locomotive is still on the departure track.** That's how it works on a real railway — you uncouple the locomotive and it runs back — so I built it that way, and it causes problems later.

*Every track carries a number, a name, and current/capacity, and you cut at the point you want with the scissors (✂) in the consist bar at the bottom.*

## 14 levels

The levels are hand-built, and each one's **par (minimum number of moves) was verified by search**. Not a single hardcoded value disagrees with the true optimum.

| Range | Wagons | par | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | 2–3 | 3–6 | Controls, LIFO, partial cuts |
| 4–7 | 4–5 | 6–9 | Using three sidings |
| 8–11 | 5–6 | 10–13 | The 3-vehicle headshunt limit |
| 12–14 | 6–8 | 14–19 | Asymmetric siding capacity |

They have names, too. First Shunt of the Morning, Two Wagons Wrong Way Round, Ballast Train, Short Headshunt, Yard at Sunset, Making Up the Last Train, Night Marshalling, The Last Train. **Time runs from morning to night.** The sun goes down as you progress, and the final levels are lit by yard lamps.

If you get stuck, a hint gives you the next move on the optimal path. To guarantee it really is optimal I run the search fresh each time, and on the last level (8 wagons) it took 1.5 seconds. The game looks frozen. I encoded the state as integers and cached a reverse index, which brought **peak blocking down to 7.9 milliseconds**.

## Building it with zero assets

This was the part of Claude of Duty I found most impressive, so I copied it directly. No image files, no sound files, no 3D model files. **Code makes all of it.**

**Textures.** Ballast lays down Voronoi cells and scatters brightness per cell. Sleepers get wood grain from directional noise and cracks from ridge noise. Wagon liveries stack panel lines, weld beads, and rivet grids as a height map over a base colour, then chip the paint at the edges so primer and rust show through, run rust streaks down from the rivets, and dust the bottom 30% with dirt. That height map goes through a Sobel filter to derive the normal map.

**Geometry.** The rail is drawn as a real 59 kg rail profile (wide foot → pinched web → mushroom head) and extruded along the track curve. Turnouts are built from actual parts — switch blades, crossing, check rails, throw bar — so the blades move when the points change. There are six wagon types, and they're **distinguishable by silhouette alone with the colour stripped out**.

**Sound.** The coupling impact runs a noise burst through three parallel bandpasses at 340 Hz, 820 Hz and 1900 Hz to make the metallic resonance, with a 60 Hz decaying sine on top for the low thud. The diesel engine is a stack of sawtooth oscillators with the cylinder firing rhythm applied as amplitude modulation. Fifteen sounds were synthesised this way.

The only dependency is **Three.js**, and even that is vendored as a single file. There's no build step. Open the HTML file and it just runs.

I put work into **the motion**, too. Every coupling has slack, so when the locomotive pulls away, each wagon only starts moving once the slack ahead of it has run out. Stopping is the reverse — the slack closes up from the back. A wave travels down the consist.

## Except three rounds never converged

That was the intent. Here's what actually happened.

I wrote a design document and handed one module each to 13 agents, all writing at once. Out came about 24,000 lines. Then I attached a "very strict art director" review agent and ran a loop: fix, resubmit, repeat until it passes.

Three rounds, 33 agents. The score moved like this:

```
Round 1  4.18 / 10
Round 2  4.92
Round 3  4.50   ← went down instead
```

### Three judges all made the same misdiagnosis

One complaint repeated across all three rounds.

> "There are no shadows. Ambient occlusion has vanished from the frame. Everything looks like it's floating."

So for three rounds I fiddled with AO radius and shadow bias. The score didn't move.

It felt wrong, so I measured it myself. I toggled each feature off and on and counted, pixel by pixel, how much the frame changed.

| Toggle | Pixels changed | Max delta |
|---|---|---|
| SSAO off → on | 19.9% | 164 |
| Shadows off → on | 12.5% | 248 |

**Both were running perfectly fine.**

The real cause was the tone curve in the final composite. I measured the histogram: `p99 = 179`, clipping 0.000%. **The brightest pixel in the scene was 70% grey.** With no pure white anywhere, all the contrast AO and shadows were producing got flattened out.

The judges **saw the symptom exactly right and got the cause wrong.** And I put that diagnosis straight into the briefing and sent 33 agents in the wrong direction.

### And nobody ever played the game

After the visual review wrapped up, I opened the game. The first reaction was this.

> "I don't know how to play this."

Followed by "how do I clear it?", "how do I get the locomotive out?", "what was the mission again?"

So I dumped every piece of text on screen.

```
LV 01 | First Shunt of the Morning | 0 / par 3 | Consist goal | 0/2 | W E
```

**The rules were nowhere.** The per-level hint text existed in the data and the tutorial function was implemented — they just never made it to the screen.

Nine review agents graded across 12 axes over three rounds. Form, materials, texture, lighting, shadows, AO, colour, composition, environment, post, UI, cohesion. **They gave UI a 6 to 7.**

Nobody asked, "can someone who doesn't know the rules play this?"

Which takes us back to that prompt. I said "review it visually," and "if it doesn't look AAA-grade, keep improving." **The agents did exactly what I asked.** Those 12 axes were a faithful transcription of the prompt, which is why all 12 of them were about looking.

What was missing wasn't the agents' diligence. It was **my question**.

I changed how I validated. I attached a "first-time player" agent, forbidden from reading the source. It had to clear the game using only screenshots and on-screen text. The moment it was in, the real problems poured out.

- The only way to uncouple a wagon was clicking a 10 px invisible sphere in 3D space. Impossible on mobile.
- The number-key mapping changed per level. On one level `4` was the departure track; on another it was the headshunt.
- Nothing told you "you have to get the locomotive out to finish," so you'd place every wagon and then be stuck.

After the fixes, two testers cleared all three levels without the source. One of them did it in par.

## Results

Once measurement split the bottlenecks apart, the rest was mechanical.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile fps (mid-range phone) | 6.9 | ~26 |
| Draw calls | 2,670 | 280 |
| Quality-switch stall | 10,299 ms | 500 ms |
| Hint response | 499 ms (and wrong) | 7.9 ms |
| One zoom step | 0.12% of the distance | 10% |

The reason draw calls were at 2,670 is that 227 grass tufts and 206 weeds were scattered as **individual meshes**, each drawn twice — once for the main pass, once for the shadow pass. Instancing them brought it to 280.

Measurement pointed the way here too. Dropping the resolution to a quarter didn't raise fps at all. That meant the problem was the CPU, not pixels, so I dug into draw calls rather than post-processing.

## What I took away

**Diagnosing took far longer than building.** Producing 24,000 lines took less than half a day. Working out "why doesn't this look good" took three days, two of which went into chasing a wrong diagnosis.

Attaching a lot of agents makes you fast. That much is true — 13 of them wrote in parallel and a game came out in a day. The problem is that **you get equally fast in the wrong direction**. One misdiagnosis, "there are no shadows," sent 33 agents to pour three days of work into the wrong place. Working alone I'd have stopped around the third attempt and said "hang on, that's odd," but an agent goes all the way, exactly as instructed.

**So measurement was everything.** Every moment this project went well was a moment I measured a number myself and put it in the briefing. "Toggling AO changes 19.9% of the frame." "Dropping to quarter resolution doesn't raise fps." One sentence like that and the round solved it. Hand over an impression like "the frame looks drab" instead, and I'd wander for rounds.

**AI review is good at finding problems and bad at diagnosing causes.** The judges were right every time they said something was wrong. They were wrong every time about *why*. Split those two apart and it's a genuinely useful tool. Don't split them, and it's expensive noise.

And the part that stung most. **One user beat nine review agents.** "I don't know how to play this" was more accurate than nine rounds of review. Until I heard that sentence, I believed the game was in a "a bit disappointing to look at, but it works as a game" state. It was the exact opposite. It was **passable to look at and broken as a game**.

One of the assessments of Claude of Duty went roughly: it runs, but it looks like a student project rather than something you'd ship. Having done this myself, I now know exactly what that means. **The distance between something that runs and something that's finished is longer than you'd think.** And that distance doesn't close with line count.

## What's left

It isn't done. Mobile boot takes 79 seconds. There's a black frame that flashes once right after boot — I know the cause and haven't fixed it. The sense of weight in the trains is about halfway there. All the sound is synthesised in code and **nobody has actually listened to it yet** — I measured the waveforms, but that's a different thing.

Still, the game runs, and someone who doesn't know the rules can clear it. That much I got to.

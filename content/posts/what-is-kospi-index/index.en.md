A new series begins. Having dug into AI's **components** in the [AI Semiconductor textbook](/en/p/what-is-hbm/) and AI's **fuel** in the [AI Power textbook](/en/p/ai-power-hunger/), this time we take on the **market itself** — the thing AI has swallowed.

Let's start with what happened last week. **On July 31, the KOSPI rose 17.91% in a single day.** It jumped 1,001 points to close at 6,595.45 — the largest single-day percentage gain and the largest point gain in 46 years. And just three days earlier, on July 28 and 29, circuit breakers had tripped on consecutive days.

Minus 8%, minus 8%, then plus 17.9%, all in one week. What produced that? Remarkably, **essentially two stocks.**

To understand that story you first need to understand what an index actually is. That's where we start today.

## KOSPI 6,000 does not mean "6,000 points"

Many people read an index like a test score. But an index is **not a score — it's a multiple.**

![How the KOSPI and KOSDAQ indices are calculated and their base dates](what-index-means.png)

The formula is surprisingly simple.

> **Index = (today's total market cap ÷ base-date total market cap) × 100**

The KOSPI's base date is **January 4, 1980.** The combined value of every listed company on that day was set to 100. So an index of 6,000 means **the market as a whole has grown 60-fold since then.** Around 6,300 today means roughly 63 times.

Knowing this changes how you read the news. "KOSPI breaks 6,000" isn't about clearing some target score — it means the market is 60 times its size of 46 years ago.

**The KOSDAQ uses a different base.** It opened on July 1, 1996 with that day set to 100, and in 2004 the base was **retroactively rescaled to 1,000** because such small index values were awkward to display.

Which produces an interesting — and slightly bleak — comparison. Two markets in the same country, and **the KOSPI has become 63 times its base while the KOSDAQ, 30 years on, still hasn't reclaimed its starting line of 1,000.** Why that is, we'll unpack over this series.

## An index is not "one stock, one vote" — it's "one won, one vote"

This is the crux. Index calculation does not give each stock an equal vote.

![The difference between equal weighting and market-cap weighting](cap-weighted.png)

The KOSPI is **market-cap weighted.** Think of it as one won of market value equaling one vote. A company worth 100 trillion won carries **100 times the voting power** of one worth 1 trillion.

Had it been equal-weighted — one vote per stock — the index would track "what the average stock is doing" far more closely. The actual KOSPI doesn't work that way. **When a large company moves, the whole index moves with it.**

Hence the familiar experience: your account is deep in the red while the news reports "KOSPI closes higher." Dozens of small caps can fall, and if one mega cap rises, the index rises. **The gap between the index and what you feel isn't an illusion — it's structural.**

## Which is why the KOSPI has already become an "AI index"

Now to the point.

![Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix as a share of KOSPI market capitalization](kospi-is-ai-index.png)

As of June 2026, **Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together account for 52.5% of KOSPI market capitalization.** Widen it to the top four names (Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, Samsung Electronics preferred, SK Square) and it reaches **61%.**

Two stocks are more than half, and **every remaining listed company combined** barely makes up the other half.

Now go one step further. **What are these two companies actually earning money from right now?** High-bandwidth memory — **HBM** — for AI servers. Semiconductor textbook [Episode 2](/en/p/hbm4-war/) covered the HBM4 war, and [Episode 3](/en/p/samsung-vs-hynix/) covered Hynix's operating profit overtaking Samsung's for the first time.

So: **half the KOSPI index is tethered to AI semiconductor earnings.**

Which explains last week's record whipsaw.

- **July 28, −8.04% and July 29, −8.17%**: China's CXMT listed successfully in Shanghai, sharpening the competitive threat, and SK Hynix missed consensus.
- **July 31, +17.91%**: strong US Big Tech results sent Samsung Electronics up 26.81% and SK Hynix to its **daily limit** — its first limit-up since the 30% price limit was adopted.

All three moves came from the same two stocks. (We'll cover that single week in detail in [Episode 3].)

## Investor's view — why this matters

**① "I bought a KOSPI ETF, so I'm diversified" may not be true.** A KOSPI tracker feels like owning hundreds of companies, but in reality **more than half of it is two AI semiconductor names.** What you thought was diversification may be a concentrated bet.

**② It's normal for the index and your account to diverge.** Under cap weighting, mega caps drag the index. If you hold small and mid caps, "the KOSPI rose but I didn't" is the expected outcome.

**③ Read index headlines one layer deeper.** When you see "KOSPI surges/plunges," get in the habit of asking **what produced it.** In today's market the answer is usually predetermined.

**④ This isn't only a Korean story.** In the US, the concentration of a handful of Big Tech names in the S&P 500 has sparked a similar debate. AI swallowing the index is closer to a global phenomenon.

## Summary

- **An index is a multiple, not a score.** KOSPI = (today's market cap ÷ market cap on January 4, 1980) × 100. At 6,000 it means 60 times the market of 46 years ago. The KOSDAQ's base is July 1, 1996 (rescaled to 1,000 in 2004) — and it still sits below that starting line after 30 years.
- **An index is one won, one vote.** Cap weighting lets large companies drive it. The gap between the index and your returns is structural, not imagined.
- **The KOSPI is already an AI index.** Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are 52.5% of market cap; the top four are 61%. And these companies earn from AI memory. **Buying the KOSPI is, in large part, betting on AI.**

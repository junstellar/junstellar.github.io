This is the most contested episode in the series. The conclusion first: **both sides have a case.**

## Suppose I sell used cars

A customer wants a car but can't get financing. No credit history. So I turn to the lender and say:

> "I'll guarantee this car is worth at least X three years from now."

The loan goes through. The customer buys the car. **I'm also the one selling it.**

I haven't lied about anything. The car is real and the customer will actually drive it. But in this transaction, **the buyer's credit is my credit.**

That is what Nvidia is doing. And **something similar happened in 1999.**

## What happened in 1999

![Vendor financing in 1999 and how it ended for Lucent](vendor-financing-then.png)

In the dot-com era, new carriers appeared everywhere promising to lay networks. They had no money. So the equipment makers stepped in: **Lucent, Cisco, Nortel.**

The method was simple. **Lend the customer money to buy equipment, and the customer buys yours.** This is **vendor financing**.

On the books it looked beautiful. The money lent came back around as **revenue**. Revenue grew, the stock rose, and the capacity to lend more grew with it.

When dot-com collapsed, the new carriers couldn't pay. Most of the loans were **written off.** And then this happened.

| Lucent share price | |
|---|---|
| End of 1999 | **$84** |
| 2002 | **55 cents** |

## What is happening in 2026

For the **Portsfike** data center campus going up in Pike County, Ohio, Nvidia has entered into **residual value guarantees of up to $105 billion** with the developer **SB Energy**, a SoftBank subsidiary. OpenAI is the tenant.

![How a residual value guarantee works](how-rvg-works.png)

A **residual value guarantee (RVG)** is this promise:

> If OpenAI cannot pay the rent, Nvidia covers part of the gap between what is recovered through re-leasing or sale and a **pre-agreed minimum value.**

Why was this needed? **OpenAI is private and has no credit rating of its own.** Without a rating, twenty-year leases are hard to get financed. So Nvidia steps in — which is why the structure is read as **OpenAI borrowing Nvidia's balance sheet as credit support.**

And one detail deserves attention. Nvidia's own disclosure states:

> **"No liability has been recorded to date, as payment is not considered probable."**

The contractual obligations from [part 2](/en/p/datacenters-off-the-books/) at least appeared in the notes. **This does not appear as a liability even there.** It sits as a contingent obligation.

### And another layer on top

Separately, Nvidia signed memoranda of understanding with six financial institutions — **Apollo Global Management, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR** — to build a funding platform mobilising **more than $500 billion** of outside capital. The aim is to make **GPUs a tradable financial asset**, the way real estate and infrastructure are.

## Except the numbers don't line up

Here I found something odd. **The figures for the size of all this differ by source.**

| What is being counted | Size |
|---|---|
| RVGs across AI firms including Meta and Broadcom | about **$70 billion** |
| Nvidia's single guarantee for the OpenAI data center | up to **$105 billion** |
| Nvidia's capital commitments to ecosystem partners | about **$300 billion** |
| The funding platform with six Wall Street firms | over **$500 billion** |
| What the press lumps together as "circular financing" | over **$750 billion** |

The industry-wide estimate is $70 billion, yet **a single transaction is $105 billion.** The counting bases and dates differ. For reference, early reporting on the OpenAI deal suggested guarantees up to $250 billion before it settled at the current size.

So this piece uses the **most concretely verifiable figure** — the $105 billion, where counterparty and asset are both named. The rest is context.

But the inconsistency itself says something. **Nobody yet has an accurate measure of how large this structure is.**

## What is the same and what is different

![What 1999 and 2026 share, and where they diverge](same-and-different.png)

### The same

- **The seller props up the buyer's funding.** The form changed from lending to guaranteeing, but the buyer still leans on the seller's credit.
- **It gets harder to tell whether demand is self-sustaining.** Demand created by money you supplied mixes with demand that wasn't.
- **Nobody can size it accurately.** As above.

### Different

**First, Lucent lent directly and booked it as revenue.** Nvidia's guarantee **creates no revenue.** No money leaves. An obligation arises later, only if certain conditions are met. Accounting-wise these are different objects.

**Second, back then the customers had no revenue.** Nvidia's position now:

| Nvidia, fiscal 2026 | |
|---|---|
| Annual revenue | **$215.9 billion** (+65%, first time past $200 billion) |
| Net income | **$42.96 billion** (+94%, record) |
| Q4 revenue | **$68.13 billion** (+73%) |

JP Morgan captured the distinction well: **"capital is chasing AI, not the other way around."** Analysts also note that today's AI companies carry far more free cash flow than dot-com era firms did.

CEO Jensen Huang pushed back directly: **"This is the same principle we apply to supply chain management — committing critical resources when we can forecast customer demand and thereby secure long-term capacity."** Nvidia's position is that this secures **infrastructure supply**, not funding demand.

**Third, they shrank it themselves.** The OpenAI deal was reported as potentially reaching $250 billion before settling at $105 billion — evidence that it isn't simply compounding unchecked.

## But does different mean better?

This is the heart of the episode.

![Who takes the loss if it goes wrong, in 1999 and now](who-holds-the-bag.png)

**When Lucent collapsed in 1999, the losses landed on Lucent and its shareholders.** The seller had carried the risk directly. Over the fall from $84 to 55 cents, that loss stayed inside one company. Brutal, but legible.

The structure now is different. **The risk has been divided across the financial system.**

BlackRock chairman Larry Fink likened this design to **the formation of the mortgage-backed securities market he witnessed in the early 1970s.** Blackstone described AI computing as a **"financeable asset, the way the mortgage market looks at housing."**

The analogy is apt. Which is exactly why it is uncomfortable.

The invention of MBS genuinely was progress — a technique for spreading risk that had been concentrated in one place. **Until 2008.**

So perhaps this is the accurate framing:

> **It is more sophisticated than 1999. The risk has not disappeared.**
> **It has moved.**

And we already saw where, in [part 2](/en/p/datacenters-off-the-books/): PIMCO, BlackRock ETFs, and pension funds. **When Lucent went wrong, Lucent's shareholders took it. If this goes wrong, it is hard to say in advance where the losses surface.**

## Where the market is placing its money

Both sides are backing their view with real money.

**The worried side**

- **Michael Burry** increased his short position in Nvidia.
- **S&P cut Oracle's credit rating from BBB to BBB-,** citing aggressive AI infrastructure expansion undermining its previously stable business profile.
- **Oracle's five-year CDS premium** rose to its highest level since the data series began at the end of 2008. A CDS is like an insurance premium against default; when it rises, the market is pricing more risk.
- The **Bank for International Settlements** warned that AI investment competition "is generating financial vulnerabilities."

**The it's-overdone side**

- **Bank of America** argues the market is **overestimating** the risk in Nvidia's ecosystem investments, seeing the stock as up to 50% undervalued.
- **JP Morgan**, while urging "a degree of caution," concludes today's deals are not the dot-com era.
- And there are the results. That $215.9 billion is not a forecast. **It has already been booked.**

## What to watch

This argument can't be settled now. Better to know **which indicators move first when it is.**

1. **Oracle's CDS premium.** It has become the thermometer for AI debt — the press calls it the "AI risk barometer."
2. **Rating actions.** Ratings move late, but when they move the signal is unambiguous. S&P's Oracle downgrade was the first.
3. **Second-hand GPU prices and rental rates.** A residual value guarantee is ultimately a bet on "what is this thing worth in three years." The useful-life debate from [part 2](/en/p/datacenters-off-the-books/) gets settled here, in money.
4. **Disclosure of guarantee amounts.** The moment the item Nvidia says is "not recorded as a liability" becomes one is the dividing line.

## Summary

- **In 1999 Lucent, Cisco and Nortel lent customers money to buy their equipment and booked it as revenue.** When dot-com collapsed most of it was written off, and Lucent went from **$84** at the end of 1999 to **55 cents** in 2002.
- **Nvidia has guaranteed up to $105 billion on the Ohio data center OpenAI will occupy.** If OpenAI can't pay rent, Nvidia covers part of the shortfall against a pre-agreed minimum value — and Nvidia disclosed that **no liability has been recorded, as payment is not considered probable.**
- **Not even the size is agreed.** $70 billion industry-wide, $105 billion for one deal, $300 billion in capital commitments, $500 billion for the platform, $750 billion for what the press calls circular financing. Different counting bases throughout.
- **It differs from 1999.** Lucent lent and booked revenue; Nvidia guarantees and books none. More importantly, those customers had no revenue, while Nvidia posted record annual revenue of **$215.9 billion** and net income of **$42.96 billion**.
- **But different doesn't mean better.** Larry Fink likened the design to the birth of the MBS market — an invention for spreading risk. **More sophisticated, yes. Gone, no.** In 1999 Lucent's shareholders absorbed it; now it sits with the bond ETFs and pension funds from part 2.
- **Watch**: Oracle's CDS, rating actions, second-hand GPU prices and rents, and the moment guarantees convert into recorded liabilities.

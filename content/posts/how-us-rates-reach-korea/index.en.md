[Part 3](/en/p/did-ai-really-raise-rates/) ended on a line: **the whole neighbourhood was dark.** And it left a question open.

> Korea's 30-year treasury yield is at a record high, and **Korea has no hyperscalers.** So why did our rates rise?

The short answer: **the neighbourhood was out, and there was also something wrong with our own fuse box.** Except the problem turned out to be somewhere I did not expect at all.

## Three countries hit records on the same day

![Korea, Japan and the US 30-year yields peaking on the same day](korea-30y-record.png)

On the day Korea's 30-year treasury reached **4.751%**, a record, Japan's 30-year hit an all-time high of **3.52%** and the US 30-year touched **5.337%**, its highest in nineteen years. **The same day.**

The shock reached our equity market days later. On 19 August the KOSPI fell **398.66 points (5.80%)** to close at **6,471.17**. A **sell-side sidecar** triggered six minutes after the open, with foreign investors selling ₩3.5 trillion and institutions ₩1.32 trillion net. The headlines called it a **"US rate shock."**

Stop there and the answer looks settled: American rates came straight through.

Except one thing was off that day. **The won actually strengthened, with the dollar down ₩14.1 to ₩1,397.7.** Money usually leaves the won when risk assets sell off; that day it didn't. The next session's rebound also came from won strength and improving flows into electronics.

A signal that this wasn't simple.

## The decisive clue — it split by maturity

![Changes by maturity since July's policy rate hike](maturity-split.png)

I applied the method from part 3: **break it up by segment.**

After the Bank of Korea raised its policy rate in mid-July, treasury yields moved like this.

| Maturity | Change |
|---|---|
| 3-year | **-5bp** (fell) |
| 10-year | +4bp |
| 30-year | **+25bp** |

**The central bank raised rates and the 3-year yield went down.** Only the 30-year jumped, by 25bp — one basis point being 0.01 percentage point.

This isn't monetary policy breaking. It means **the jurisdictions are different.**

> **The short end is set by the Bank of Korea. The long end is set by the world.**

The policy rate directly moves the very shortest rates, and that influence carries out to the 3-year. But a yield looking thirty years ahead is set by **the global term premium** — the one from part 3.

So "did US rates come through" was only half right. **They didn't come through. It was always the same market.**

## But why did only the 30-year jump so much?

This is where the episode surprised me.

If there were a domestic factor, I assumed it would be **the fiscal deficit or bond supply** — that's what part 3 found in America. The real reason was elsewhere.

![The distortion insurer demand created, and its unwinding](why-only-30y.png)

### Insurers had been buying the 30-year

With **IFRS 17** and **K-ICS** (the new solvency regime), insurers began marking insurance liabilities **to market**. Insurance contracts run very long. To keep valuations from swinging, assets have to run long too. That is **duration matching**.

So insurers bought ultra-long government bonds in volume. The scale:

- **30.2% of all treasury issuance in 2024** was 30-year paper.
- Between end-2014 and end-2024, roughly **half (47.9%) of the increase in outstanding treasuries — ₩291 trillion — was 30-year.**

The result was something rare globally: **since 2017, Korea's 30-year yield sat below its 10-year yield.**

Normally you get paid more for lending longer — that is the term premium. Korea was inverted. **When a class of buyers has to buy, prices distort.**

### Now that demand has faded

Insurers' incentive to hold ultra-long paper has weakened. The traditional buyer stepped back while issuance kept coming. Yields rise.

Citi called this **"ultra-long curve normalisation"** — something compressed returning to its natural shape.

Which means the accurate framing is this. **Korea's 30-year hitting a record is partly because it had been abnormally low.** America's record and Korea's record share a name but not a cause: in the US a premium appeared that wasn't there; in Korea **a suppressed one is being released.**

### And one common explanation is wrong

When yields rise, people usually say **"foreigners sold."** Not this time.

**Foreign investors were net buyers of won bonds** — including on the day the 30-year hit its record. Passive money keeps arriving through Korea's inclusion in the **World Government Bond Index (WGBI).**

Analysts do note that even with foreign inflows, **the supply-demand structure of the won bond market remains fragile.** When buyers were concentrated in one type and that type retreats, new money doesn't easily fill the gap.

The phrase **"bond vigilantes"** also surfaced in this stretch — investors who sell government bonds to push yields up and pressure a government they judge fiscally loose, a term coined during the Clinton administration.

## Meanwhile the Bank of Korea raised rates

On 27 August the Monetary Policy Board lifted the policy rate from **2.75% to 3.00%**, a **second consecutive hike**, with six of seven members in favour.

The accompanying forecasts drew more attention.

| | Previous | Now |
|---|---|---|
| 2026 GDP growth forecast | 2.6% | **3.3%** |
| CPI inflation forecast | 2.7% | 2.7% (unchanged) |

A **0.7 percentage point** upgrade to growth. It read as moving before prices do, and the published dot plot's highest of 21 dots was **3.5%**.

So Korean monetary policy is in a **tightening cycle**. As we saw, though, that is mostly a short-end story.

## So how does it reach my account?

![The four channels through which rates reach an individual](four-gates.png)

### 1. Fixed-rate mortgages — within days

Hybrid (fixed) mortgages price off **five-year bank bonds**, which track government yields. Their recent surge pushed **hybrid mortgage rates into the 5% range.**

**US 30-year → Korean treasury → five-year bank bond → my loan rate.** That chain is this episode's title.

### 2. Floating-rate mortgages — next month

Floating rates price off **COFIX**, a weighted average of eight banks' funding costs; when it rises, floating mortgages follow the next month. The new-lending COFIX has now risen **two months running.**

### 3. Equity discount rates — same day

The fastest channel. Higher rates shrink the present value of future earnings, and growth stocks — priced on distant profits — move first. The KOSPI's 5.80% fall on 19 August was exactly this. The discount-rate-and-PER relationship from [part 6 of the index series](/en/p/reading-index-ai-era/) at work.

### 4. Deposit rates — slowly

The policy rate rose, yet **deposit rates are noted as lagging.** Lending rates and deposit rates don't move at the same speed. Of the four gates, this is the one that doesn't open easily.

## Answering part 3's question

> **Did US rates transmit straight through, or is something domestic at work?**

**Both — but you have to separate them.**

- **Short end (3-year)**: Bank of Korea territory. It actually fell 5bp despite a hike.
- **Long end (30-year)**: set by the global term premium. Korea, Japan and the US peaking on the same day is the evidence.
- **Plus one reason unique to Korea**: a distortion built by insurer demand is unwinding. Hence 30-year alone, +25bp.

Part 3 concluded AI was one of three culprits and probably the smallest. **By the time you get to Korea, AI's share shrinks further.** The biggest force behind our 30-year yield wasn't AI bonds or foreign selling. It was **an accounting regime's demand disappearing.**

## Summary

- **Korea (4.751%), Japan (3.52%) and the US (5.337%) hit 30-year records the same day.** Days later the KOSPI fell 5.80% to 6,471.17, with a sidecar six minutes after the open.
- **It split by maturity.** After July's hike: 3-year **-5bp**, 10-year +4bp, 30-year **+25bp**. **The short end is the central bank's; the long end is the world's.**
- **The 30-year had a Korean reason.** After IFRS 17 and K-ICS, insurers bought ultra-long paper heavily enough that **the 30-year yielded less than the 10-year from 2017**. That demand is fading and the curve is normalising — Citi's term.
- **"Foreigners sold" is wrong.** They were net buyers, with WGBI passive money still arriving — though the structure is called fragile.
- **On 27 August the policy rate went to 3.00%,** a second straight hike, with the growth forecast raised from 2.6% to **3.3%**.
- **Four gates reach your account**: fixed mortgages (five-year bank bonds, now 5%+), floating mortgages (COFIX up two months), equity discount rates (same day), and deposit rates (slowly).

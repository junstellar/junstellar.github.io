The last episode of the series.

I wrote [Episode 1](/en/p/what-is-sector-rotation/) on August 12, in the middle of the rotation. As I write this, **that rotation is already over.**

## It lasted three weeks

![The full arc of the rotation as seen in the KOSDAQ — opening, peak, close](rotation-window.png)

| | KOSDAQ | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| August 3 | 737.35 | KOSPI -5.12% but KOSDAQ +2.44%. **The window opened** |
| August 10 | **854.47** | 1,431 stocks up, 11 limit-ups, buy-side sidecar. **The peak** |
| August 21 | **801.94** | -4.63%, sell-side sidecar, 1,378 stocks down. **It closed** |

On August 21 the KOSDAQ opened at 824.05, fell to 795.57 intraday, and triggered a **sell-side sidecar at 10:05 a.m.** What broke that day were precisely the sectors this series has been covering.

- Defence — Hanwha Aerospace **-7.03%**
- Biotech — Alteogen -5.60%, ABL Bio -5.09%
- Batteries — EcoPro BM -4.91%, LG Energy Solution -4.05%
- Shipbuilding — HD Hyundai Heavy -4.73%
- Robotics — Rainbow Robotics -3.92%

Samsung Electronics, meanwhile, rose **3.87%** and SK Hynix 2.31%.

This is where I have to test my own conclusion from [Episode 3](/en/p/why-shipbuilding-defense-rise/). I argued that shipbuilding and defence run on different engines. Yet on this day they **fell together.** A contradiction?

No — it's the property from [Episode 2](/en/p/when-the-leader-rests/). **When money is leaving, correlations rise.** On ordinary days each sector runs on its own engine; when the money goes, it goes from all of them. And the cause was the one from [Episode 4](/en/p/is-bio-a-rate-play/): US long yields and oil rose together, widening the discount-rate burden, on top of profit-taking after the previous day's surge.

That said, **the KOSDAQ is still positive for August as a whole** — 719.76 on July 31 to 801.94 on August 21, **+11.42%**, against +4.81% for the KOSPI. It rose, and it has also already rolled over **6.15%** from its August 10 high. Both are true.

## And yet the index showed nothing

The reason this episode exists is contained in that single session.

![On August 21 the KOSPI rose while 683 stocks fell](index-hides-the-market.png)

The **KOSPI rose 0.88%** that day. From the headline, a quiet session.

But on the KOSPI, **193 stocks rose and 683 fell** — decliners outnumbered advancers **3.5 to 1**. The index rose because Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix carried it.

On the KOSDAQ: 282 up, **1,378 down.** Only **17%** of stocks rose.

And just **eleven sessions earlier**, on August 10, 1,431 KOSDAQ stocks rose — over **80%** of the market — with 11 hitting the daily limit.

**In eleven sessions, the share of rising stocks went from 80% to 17%.** None of that is written anywhere in the index. A market-cap-weighted index goes up when the big names carry it, even if everything else falls — the structure covered in [Index Episode 1](/en/p/what-is-kospi-index/).

**Try to track a rotation with the index and you'll miss both the start and the end.**

## So here are five things to watch

Only the indicators that actually answered something across these six episodes.

![Five indicators for reading a rotation, with their August readings](five-indicators.png)

### ① Investor deposits — is the money growing or shrinking?

Cash sitting in brokerage accounts waiting to buy. **If the index rises while this grows, new money arrived. If it rises while this shrinks, the money that stayed crowded into a narrower space.**

That was Episode 1's finding exactly. From a record 139.7 trillion won on June 4 to 97.9 trillion on August 11 — **down nearly 30% while the KOSDAQ rose 19%.** It has since recovered to 100.1 trillion (August 13) and 104.8 trillion (August 18).

### ② Margin loans and forced selling — has the liquidation finished?

Money borrowed to buy. [Episode 2](/en/p/when-the-leader-rests/) found the rotation window's real precondition here: KOSDAQ margin loans fell from 11.05 trillion won at end-April to 5.83 trillion on August 3. **Only once the must-sell supply was gone did the market start choosing again.**

Watch the **forced-sale ratio** alongside it. The Korea Financial Investment Association publishes it daily with unsettled receivables, and practitioners read **above 5% as a capitulation signal.** On July 30–31 it hit 8.6% and 7.1% — the two days circuit breakers fired.

**Note that margin loans are climbing again.** From 28.9 trillion won on July 31 to 31.89 trillion as of August 20. The condition that opened the window is reversing.

### ③ Market breadth — how many stocks are actually rising?

The numbers above. Advancers and decliners. Accumulate them over 20 days and you get the **ADR (advance-decline ratio)**.

> ADR = 20-day sum of advancers ÷ 20-day sum of decliners × 100
> 100% = balance · **above 120% = overheated** · **below 70% = near a bottom**

Those thresholds are long-standing **practitioner convention** rather than academically validated levels. Reading the *direction* is safer than the number. If the index is flat while advancers grow, something is moving underneath. If the index rises while advancers shrink, that rise is leaning on a handful of names.

### ④ Relative strength — which side is leading?

KOSPI versus KOSDAQ, large caps versus small. The calculation is simply putting the two returns side by side.

August was KOSDAQ **+11.42%** versus KOSPI **+4.81%**. But over the year it ran the other way, as [Episode 2](/en/p/when-the-leader-rests/) showed: from January to July, days when the KOSPI rose and the KOSDAQ fell hit 32 of 132 sessions (24%) — **the highest annual share since the KOSDAQ opened.**

For reference, KOSPI large caps are the top 100 by market value, mid caps the next 200, small caps the next 514, rebalanced twice a year (the day after the March and September futures-options expiries).

### ⑤ Flows — who is buying and who is selling?

The most-watched and most-misread indicator. The classic error is **judging from a single day.**

In August, foreigners bought 3 trillion won in one session and sold 4 trillion the following week. A single day tells you nothing. **Use 5-day and 20-day cumulative figures.**

Also worth watching is the **"other corporations"** line — not institutions, foreigners or individuals, but ordinary companies. Flows unrelated to fundamentals show up here, like buybacks or post-IPO block sales. On August 21 this category bought over 1 trillion won net, driven by SK Hynix's buyback.

## "Isn't checking always too late?"

A fair objection, and half right.

But what these six episodes actually found is that **the people trying to predict the rotation were mostly wrong too.** In July most Korean brokerages named shipbuilding and defence as the next leaders; what rose most in August was biotech — and that rolled over within three weeks.

So what I want to leave isn't a forecast but an order of operations.

1. **Look at breadth before the index.** How many stocks are rising is more honest than where the index closed.
2. **Check the total.** Whether deposits are growing or shrinking makes the same rally mean two entirely different things.
3. **Don't buy it all at once.** The lateness problem isn't solved by prediction; it's solved by **splitting entries.** As [Episode 5](/en/p/why-retail-took-the-other-side/) showed, turnover and instrument mattered more than direction.

One principle from Park Se-ik of Chesley Investment Advisory helps here: **if the cause of a decline isn't damage to the company's competitiveness, averaging in works; if the fundamentals themselves have broken, cutting is right.** The question to answer is not "how far has it fallen" but "why."

## So where are we now?

Honestly — **nobody knows yet.** Views diverge.

Yeom Seung-hwan of LS Securities leans toward the rotation continuing: **"the intensity of semiconductor concentration will ease in the second half and the warmth will spread across sectors."** Daishin Securities describes the market as **"shifting from a low-valuation, low-volatility regime to an earnings-sensitive one."**

Shin Seung-jin of Samsung Securities offers a diagnosis that lands exactly on Episode 1's finding:

> **"With retail buying power limited, semiconductor leadership and the KOSDAQ index have no choice but to decouple."**

When the money is finite, one side rising means the other gets left out. Which is the same thing as "shrinking money crowded into a narrower space."

Two things to check this week: **Nvidia's earnings early on August 27** Korean time (consensus revenue of $91.9 billion) and the **Bank of Korea's rate decision the same day** — the first meeting since July's hike from 2.50% to 2.75%.

## Summary

- **This rotation lasted three weeks.** It opened August 3, peaked August 10, and closed August 21. The KOSDAQ is +11.42% for August but -6.15% from its high.
- **The index hides the market.** On August 21 the KOSPI rose 0.88% while 683 stocks fell, and only 17% of KOSDAQ stocks rose — against 80% eleven sessions earlier.
- **Five indicators suffice.** Investor deposits, margin loans and forced selling, market breadth, relative strength, cumulative flows. All free.
- **Never read flows from one day.** Use 5- and 20-day sums. Foreigners bought 3 trillion one week and sold 4 trillion the next.
- **The lateness problem is better solved by splitting entries than by forecasting.** The forecasters were mostly wrong too.

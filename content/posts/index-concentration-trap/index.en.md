In [Episode 1](/en/p/what-is-kospi-index/) we saw that an index is "one won, one vote" — which means buying the KOSPI isn't diversification so much as a concentrated bet on AI semiconductors.

Today we look at **how far that concentration has gone**, and **how other countries handle it.** I'll say upfront: the last section genuinely surprised me.

## The speed is the alarming part

Start with the numbers.

![Trend of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix as a share of KOSPI market cap](concentration-speed.png)

By the Korea Capital Market Institute's count, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix went from **23% of KOSPI market capitalization in early 2025 to 55% by the end of June 2026** — more than double in eighteen months. (Different sources put it anywhere from 51% to 56%, but the direction is identical.)

**Inside the KOSPI 200 it's starker.** As of August 2026, Samsung Electronics is 33.1% and SK Hynix 26.5%, for a combined **59.6%**. The third-largest name is under 3%. It's a benchmark built from 200 stocks, and two of them effectively decide it.

## What "the index rose but my stocks fell" actually looks like

Episode 1 argued that the gap between the index and your experience is structural, not imagined. The receipts exist.

![Days when the index rose while far more stocks declined than advanced](index-illusion.png)

- **May 27, 2026**: the KOSPI closed higher — with **75 stocks up and 826 down.** Eleven to one.
- **June 2026, on a day the index hit a record high**: 210 up, **688 down.**

The benchmark set records while most of the market fell. That is precisely the illusion cap weighting produces.

Which led to a striking estimate. In a June 2026 report titled "Shadow of the Semiconductor Giant," Eugene Investment & Securities analyst Heo Jae-hwan put the **KOSPI excluding semiconductors at roughly 4,100–4,200.** Semiconductors' share of market cap had jumped from 25% to 54.6% in a year, and he expects them to account for 60–70% of total KOSPI operating profit.

The valuation angle is the interesting part. The KOSPI's 12-month forward P/E of 8.1x looks cheap — but **excluding semiconductors it rises to 11x**, above the post-COVID average of 10.4x. In other words, "Korea is cheap" may itself be an artifact of semiconductors.

## The side effects have already arrived

This isn't a theoretical worry.

Per the Capital Market Institute, daily return volatility on the KOSPI in the first half of 2026 was **3.6%, more than double the prior year's 1.4%.** March and June came in at 4.8% and 4.7% — **higher than March 2020 (4.2%), the depths of the COVID crash.** In the first half alone there were 85 market stabilization measures and 13 circuit breakers.

The mechanism is clear. The two stocks have a **daily return correlation of 0.82** — they move nearly as one. When two near-identical names are more than half the index, any shock to the memory cycle swings the whole market.

Kim Jun-seok, senior research fellow at the Capital Market Institute, framed it this way: **"With a handful of stocks holding an excessive share of the index, institutional profit-taking colliding with retail chasing amplifies even small shocks into market-wide volatility."**

For context, these two names also exceed 55% of the National Pension Service's domestic equity portfolio. This isn't only a retail problem.

## But is this a uniquely Korean disease?

Here we need balance. Before filing concentration under "Korea discount," look at everyone else.

![Index concentration compared across Korea, Taiwan, Switzerland, the US and Denmark](global-comparison.png)

- **Taiwan**: TSMC alone is 44–47% of the TAIEX.
- **Switzerland**: Novartis, Roche and Nestlé together exceed 60%.
- **United States**: the S&P 500's top 10 are 37–43%, and by some measures **more concentrated than at the dot-com peak** — back then 32 stocks made up half the market cap; today 24 do.
- **Denmark**: Novo Nordisk and Ørsted together approach 30%.

So **index concentration in the AI era is a global phenomenon.** The degree differs, though. Taiwan is "one stock near half"; Korea is **"two stocks past half"** — which puts Korea among the most extreme in reliance on a tiny handful of names.

Denmark is worth reading as a warning. When Novo Nordisk fell 48% in 2025, the OMXC25 became the worst-performing developed benchmark in the world. One stock dragged down a country's entire index.

## What surprised me — the fix exists, and Korea went the other way

This is the part I didn't expect.

![Weight cap rules in major indices compared with the KOSPI 200](cap-rules.png)

Most major indices impose a **cap on individual weights.** Denmark's OMXC25 caps at 7%, Japan's Nikkei 225 at 10%, the Swiss SMI at about 18%, the NYSE 100 at 20%, and the Nasdaq 100 at 24%.

Caps genuinely work. In 2023 the Nasdaq 100 ran a **special rebalance** that cut the combined weight of seven Big Tech names from **55% to 43% in a single day.**

**The KOSPI 200 has no cap.**

And not merely by omission. In 2020, as Samsung Electronics' weight grew, there was a public debate over a "30% cap rule" — after which the market-cap weight ceiling for the KOSPI 200 and KRX 300 was **abolished**, in the name of easing fund-management regulation.

Six years on, those two stocks are 59.6% of the KOSPI 200.

Taiwan, for what it's worth, has moved the same direction: with TSMC so large, regulators **raised** the single-stock limit for funds from 10% to 25%. Unable to hold the cap, they lifted it. The world is splitting into two camps, and Korea and Taiwan are on the same side.

## The other side deserves a hearing

That concentration is a problem is not unanimous.

**KB Securities analyst Lee Eun-taek** argues that leadership concentration is natural, and that **the moment concentration unwinds is itself the leading symptom of a bubble bursting.** In 1929, in the Nifty Fifty of 1972, and in the 2000 dot-com bubble, the leaders also showed genuinely rapid profit growth — much like semiconductors now.

Oxford's Professor Mungo Wilson notes that Samsung and Hynix earnings are read not as single-company news but as **signals about global AI investment and chip demand**, making their sway over the index natural. Goldman Sachs likewise calls it earnings-driven — "profits are pulling Asian equities."

So why not just use an **equal-weighted index**? Not so simple either. The US equal-weight S&P 500 ETF (RSP) gained 12.78% in 2024 against the cap-weighted index's 21.45%, and 11.20% versus 15.54% in 2025. In a concentration-led rally, equal weighting costs you. Its turnover also runs more than five times higher, and so do trading costs.

**My own read:** concentration alone isn't a peak signal, and the earnings are real. But Heo Jae-hwan's phrasing seems right — **"concentration itself isn't bad news, but it isn't healthy."** And the point of this episode is that other countries keep a tool to moderate that unhealthy state, while Korea removed its own.

## Investor's view — four points

**① Check whether you own an index or two stocks.** If you hold a KOSPI 200 ETF, roughly 60% of it is Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. Buy those two separately on top and your real exposure is far larger.

**② Remember the 0.82 correlation.** The two move almost as one, so splitting between them buys you little diversification.

**③ Question "Korea is cheap."** That 8.1x forward P/E is a number semiconductors produced. Strip them out and it's 11x — above the post-COVID average.

**④ Watch for rule changes.** Analysts are now floating a weight cap for the KOSPI 200. If one arrives, rebalancing would force simultaneous selling of mega caps and buying of smaller names. An index rule change is itself a flow event.

## Summary

- **The concentration is accelerating.** Samsung Electronics plus SK Hynix went from 23% of KOSPI market cap in early 2025 to 55% by mid-2026, and they are **59.6% of the KOSPI 200.** The third-largest name is under 3%.
- **The illusion is measurable.** On a day the index rose, 826 stocks fell. Excluding semiconductors, the index is estimated near 4,100. Volatility now exceeds the COVID crash period.
- **Concentration is global; the responses are not.** Denmark caps at 7%, the Nikkei at 10%, Switzerland at 18%, the Nasdaq 100 at 24%. **The KOSPI 200 has no cap — and the rule it once had was abolished in 2020.**
